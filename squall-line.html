Squall Line

Metal, fronted by Chase Liberty

Hear the story

Squall Line started with a song that wouldn't sit still. "Orders or the Oath" began life as a folk tune, the same register as everything on Walking Home, but it came out too overtly subversive for the genre and got shelved. Months later, revisiting it with a heavier sound, the song changed completely: the rage came through clearer and sharper, and it did something a folk arrangement couldn't, it drove the listener to move, even if that just meant air guitar or drumming on the steering wheel. Squall Line is the same voice behind Chase Liberty, fronting a metal band instead of a folk record, because some things just need to be loud.

Album: Eye of the Storm

Recording — release date TBA

Eye of the Storm grew out of that realization, built from the ground up around songs that needed more weight than an acoustic guitar could carry. It's still being recorded, three songs in so far, each one driven by the same thread: conscience under pressure, and rage turned into something you can act on.

In May of 2026, the President of the United States sued his own IRS for ten billion dollars over the 2019 leak of his tax returns. He filed it in his personal capacity. His own Justice Department argued the other side. Months later, with the judge days away from ruling on whether the whole thing was even a real case, he dropped the suit in exchange for an apology, a $1.776 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund” paid out of the Treasury Judgment Fund, and — attached the next day, after the headlines had moved on — a waiver that forever barred the IRS from pursuing any pre-May-eighteenth tax matter involving him, his sons, or his businesses.

That number is not an accident. 1.776 billion. The same four digits we were raised to associate with a declaration that no man is a king.

I didn't want to be subtle about this one. I also didn't want to just scream a name at the audience. Nobody needs me to tell them who the king is. So I built the whole song around a courthouse and a throne room and let the listener do the pointing.

The hook came in one piece, before anything else: only guilty men ask for immunity. Everything else had to earn its place against that line. The first chorus stays with the court itself — he holds the court and the key, he calls captivity our gain. The second moves to the immunity clause as a key of its own, unlocking “every cell he knew he'd see.” That verb matters. Not feared. Knew.

The whole song is loud, and I needed the loudest moment to be the quietest one. In the bridge, the band drops out completely and the acoustic reads the waiver into the record like a verdict: “Forever barred. Forever precluded.” Then the question nobody in the settlement had to answer: “verdicts come from juries, the jury wasn't there.” The build out of that silence asks the only honest follow-up — so tell me again how you have nothing to hide, when the only safe room is the ballroom locked from the inside.

Musically, this one borrows as much from noir jazz as it does from metal, which isn't a pairing we'd tried before. A muted, smoky horn line runs underneath the verses like a courtroom score, brushed-sounding percussion standing in for someone pacing a hallway, before the full weight of the band slams back in for the chorus. It's an odd combination on paper, but it fit the subject: something cool and watchful under all that rage, like the band itself is building a case.

The song ends on “we'll pry it from his tiny hand.” I know. But if we're going to be honest about who we're singing to, we may as well be honest about his hands.

This one started life as a folk song, with some glitch and dub textures under it. Immigration enforcement was ramping up, and I kept thinking about the officers being sent to the doors. Every one of them swore an oath at some point, and that oath was to a document, not to a person. So what does a decent cop do when the orders and the oath stop pointing the same way? The first version asked that question gently. It had a kid's drawing on the fridge, a badge getting heavier, and a chorus that turned the question back on the listener: where do you draw your line?

When we started building the metal record, it was the first song I wanted to run back through the machine. A person's conscience pushing against an institution is exactly what this band is for. The acoustic guitar is the person, and everything behind them is the weight bearing down.

The first metal pass didn't work. It sounded big, but it had a crowd singing the chorus, and the whole thing came out like a beer commercial. A song about one person standing alone can't be sung by a crowd. The group vocals came out that day and haven't been back on this album since.

The bigger change came when I realized the song was still too polite. They weren't wrestling with their conscience anymore. They'd been the good one, they'd believed every word, and that belief was used against them. That isn't a moral debate. That's betrayal. So we threw out almost everything except the central question and one line I wouldn't give up: “Constitution or command, which one's clearer?”

The rewrite starts in the middle of the storm, with no quiet intro to ease you in. The acoustic doesn't lay the foundation this time. It cuts in, like their conscience showing up late. Then the band drops out completely and it's just their voice and the guitar: “I'm taking my hands back.” That's the moment they decide. The original chorus asked where you draw your line. By the last chorus they aren't asking anymore: “Here is where I draw my line.”

It ends twice. The first time is quiet, almost a statement for the record: the oath was to the Constitution, “not to one man's command.” Then the band comes back and says it again at full force, and one guy in the band shouts the last word with him. One voice, not a crowd. That felt like the right amount of company for a decision like that.

On October 15th, a sixteen-year-old shot his way through the locked doors of Silver Rail Elementary in Bend, here in Oregon. Three children and a teacher didn't go home. A man who saw him go in followed him inside, carrying a gun of his own, and the shooting stopped there.

That's what I came for. What I couldn't get past was everything around it. A roller coaster had come off its rails. A bank paid a fine and nobody went to jail. Social Security went up by a few dollars. An ambassador resigned in a misconduct scandal. Federal agents searched a congresswoman's home. The President flew to a rally in a jet a foreign royal family gave him. Somewhere in Yemen, 800,000 people were cut off from food. The House race map scrolled across the bottom of the screen the whole time. Every one of those got the same voice, the same smile and the same thirty seconds before the break.

That's the song. It isn't about one tragedy. It's about a news cycle that puts dead children and a stock dip on the same shelf and calls it a day.

My first instinct was to tell it gently, the way a folk singer would. It came out sounding like a country singer standing in front of a metal band, which was exactly wrong. You don't sing this one politely. So it kept getting faster, heavier and angrier until it sounded like what it was about.

A few choices mattered more than the rest. Silver Rail sits in the third verse, buried between a market panic and a missile strike, because that's where the news put it. I wanted that to sting. I also wanted to be honest about the man who stopped the shooter without turning it into a hero story: “Two armed men through the same school door.” Both of them carried a gun through that door, and the song leaves that there without telling you how to feel about it.

The song only slows down once, in the bridge, for the person the cameras never show: “Somewhere a mother waits by the phone, / For a name that's never coming home.”

Then it ends the way the broadcast does: “That's the news, good night. We'll do it all again tomorrow.” Except here it's screamed, because the worst part isn't that it happened. It's that tomorrow it will happen again.
